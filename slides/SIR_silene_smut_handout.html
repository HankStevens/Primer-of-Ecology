Is Anther Smut Density- or Frequency-Dependent? Ask the Data

Ch. 12: SIR disease models · Silene and anther smut in roadside populations, Virginia · Work in pairs

The system

White campion (Silene latifolia) is a short-lived roadside wildflower. It can be infected by the anther-smut fungus Microbotryum, which replaces pollen with fungal spores and sterilizes the plant. Pollinators carry the spores from flower to flower, so the disease spreads much like a sexually transmitted infection. Janis Antonovics and colleagues surveyed hundreds of roadside segments near Mountain Lake Biological Station, Virginia, counting healthy (H) and diseased (D) plants in each one. Each segment is one population, with N = H + D plants.

Part A. What does each model predict?

Use the I zero net growth isocline (dI/dt = 0) from class. Let γ be the rate at which diseased plants are lost from the population.

DD: βSI − γI = 0  ⇒  S* = γ/β  ⇒  prevalence I*/N = 1 − (γ/β)/N
FD: βSI/N − γI = 0  ⇒  S*/N = γ/β  ⇒  prevalence I*/N = 1 − γ/β

Part D. Interpret

  1. Look at prevalence in populations larger than about 20 plants. DD predicts prevalence rising with N; equilibrium FD predicts it staying flat. Which prediction do the data reject? Do the data match the other one exactly? If not, suggest a biological reason prevalence might fall with N. (Hint: consider the natural history of this system: How is the disease spread? How are these Silene populations arranged on the landscape? Might populations change over time? Consider how the proportion of populations that are infected varies with average population size. Does that give you any ideas?)
  2. Estimate R0. If transmission is FD and populations are near equilibrium, prevalence = 1 − γ/β = 1 − 1/R0, so R0 = 1 / (1 − prevalence). Use the prevalence of the largest class:  R0 ≈ . What does a value this close to 1 tell you about the disease?
  3. Your R0 assumed populations are at equilibrium. If the disease were still spreading into large populations, would the true R0 be higher or lower than your estimate?
  4. Could it be an artifact? We calculated prevalence only for populations with at least one diseased plant. What is the lowest possible non-zero prevalence in a population of 30 plants?  Of 800 plants?  Explain how this floor alone could make prevalence appear to fall with N, even with no biological cause.
