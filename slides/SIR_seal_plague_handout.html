Seal Plague: Estimating Epidemic Rates in a Wildlife Population

Ch. 12: SIR disease models · Harbor seals, Swedish Kattegat, 2002 · Work in pairs; you need a calculator with ln

The outbreak

In May 2002, dead harbor seals (Phoca vitulina) began washing ashore on the island of Anholt in the Kattegat, between Denmark and Sweden. The cause was phocine distemper virus (PDV), a morbillivirus related to measles and canine distemper. PDV had last swept through these colonies in 1988, so almost every seal alive in 2002 was susceptible. Over the next months the epidemic spread to colonies across northern Europe and killed thousands of seals.

Wildlife biologists rarely get to count infected animals; seals at sea cannot be tested. What they can count is carcasses. The back page gives the weekly cumulative number of dead seals recovered on the Swedish Kattegat coast, starting with the first week deaths were found there.

Key idea. Infected seals die about one infectious period after they are infected, so the number dying each week rises and falls in step with the number infected, I, just shifted later in time. That means deaths grow and decline at the same exponential rates as I, and we can read SIR rates from carcass counts.

Part A. Turn cumulative deaths into new deaths

New deaths in week t = cumulative(t) − cumulative(t − 1). Fill in that column, then ln(cumulative) and ln(new). Plot both ln columns against week on the grid, using different symbols.

Part B. Growth rate r, from the start of the epidemic

Early on, S ≈ N, so dI/dt ≈ (βN − γ)I = rI, and I grows exponentially. Cumulative deaths grow at the same rate r early on, and they are less noisy, so use ln(cumulative). Draw a straight line through the early points where ln(cumulative) rises steeply in a straight line. Its slope is r (per week):

r = (ln Clater − ln Cearlier) / (weeks between them)

Part C. From r to R0: you need to know how long seals stay infectious

Because r = βN − γ and R0 = βN/γ,

R0 = 1 + r/γ = 1 + r × D,    where D = 1/γ is the infectious period

Nobody measured D for wild seals, so calculate R0 for several plausible values of D (back page). The same growth rate r gives very different R0 values depending on D.

Part D. Use the end of the epidemic to put a limit on D

Late in the epidemic, dI/dt = (βS − γ)I. The per capita rate of decline is γ − βS, and because βS can't be negative, the observed rate of decline can be no larger than γ. Use ln(new deaths) on the falling side of the curve, after the peak:

decline rate = (ln newearlier − ln newlater) / (weeks between)  ≤  γ     so     D = 1/γ ≤ 1 / (decline rate)

This gives you an upper limit on D, and therefore on R0, straight from the data.
