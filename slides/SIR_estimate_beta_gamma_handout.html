Estimating β and γ from an Epidemic

Ch. 12: SIR disease models · Influenza in an English boarding school, January 1978 · Work in pairs; you need a calculator with ln

The outbreak

In January 1978 one boy returned from holiday to a boarding school in northern England carrying a new strain of influenza (H1N1). Over the next two weeks the flu swept through the school. There were N = 763 boys in a closed population: no one entered or left. The school nurse recorded B, the number of boys confined to bed each day (back page). In all, 512 boys were sick at some point.

We will treat B as the number infected, I, and fit the density-dependent SIR model:

dS/dt = −βSI      dI/dt = βSI − γI      dR/dt = γI

Your goal: estimate the recovery rate γ, the transmission coefficient β, and R0, using only the data table and a pencil.

Part A. Recovery rate γ, from how long boys stay sick

Recall that γ = 1/D, where D is the average duration of infection. Each sick boy adds 1 to B for every day he spends in bed. So if you add up B over all days, you get the total number of boy-days spent in bed.

D = (total boy-days in bed) / (number of boys who were sick) = ΣB / 512     and     γ = 1/D

Part B. Growth rate r, from the start of the outbreak

At the start, almost everyone is susceptible, so S ≈ N and the model becomes

dI/dt ≈ (βN − γ) I = r I     so     I(t) = I0ert     and     ln I(t) = ln I0 + r t

This is exponential growth, so ln(B) plotted against day should be a straight line early on, with slope r. Fill in the ln(B) column, plot ln(B) vs. day, and draw a straight line by eye through the early points where the curve is straight. Its slope is r:

r = (ln Blater − ln Bearlier) / (number of days between them)

Part C. Put them together: β and R0

Since r = βN − γ, rearrange to get

β = (r + γ) / N       and       R0 = βN/γ = 1 + r/γ

Part D. A second, independent estimate of R0: the final size

The SIR model predicts what fraction of the population, z, is ever infected: z = 1 − e−R0z. Solving for R0:

R0 = −ln(1 − z) / z,     where z = 512/763

Part E. Do your two estimates agree?

If the model were a perfect description of the school, Parts C and D would give the same R0. On the back, compare them and think about which assumptions of the SIR model might not hold here.
